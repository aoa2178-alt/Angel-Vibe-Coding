# Everything has a structure underneath

_Personal · Mathematics — Why I studied mathematics · 3 min read_

> I studied mathematics because every problem had a structure underneath, and if you looked carefully enough, you could find it.

People assume a mathematics degree means you love equations. I want to be precise, I do but the tick I get goes beyond that. I loved the theorems and proofs more because of the process of taking something complex or ambiguous, breaking it into components, working out how those components relate, and reasoning toward an answer that holds.

The pleasure was never in calculating. It was in the click — the moment the logic stops being a sequence of steps you are following and becomes a thing you can see whole. You turn a problem over for an hour, and then it resolves, and afterwards you cannot quite reconstruct why it was ever difficult. That moment is the whole reason I kept going.

The other thing I loved was stranger, and harder to explain to people who did not study it: the ability to mentally visualize something so abstract. Mathematics asks you to hold objects that do not exist in any physical sense — a space you cannot draw, a relationship with no shape, an infinity you are somehow expected to reason about — and to develop an intuition for them anyway. You start with symbols on a page, and at some point they stop being symbols and start being something you can move around in your head. Learning that I could build a mental image of pure abstraction changed what I believed I was capable of understanding.

> An answer isn't right because it sounds convincing. The reasoning has to hold.

My favourite method was always proof by contradiction. You want to show something is true, so you assume the opposite, follow it honestly wherever it goes, and keep going until it collapses into an impossibility. The thing you were trying to prove is then left standing, not because you argued for it, but because the alternative could not survive.

I find I still work that way. Take the explanation you have been handed, assume it is right, and follow it all the way out. If it holds, good. But often it doesn't — the numbers stop reconciling, two things that were supposed to be consistent aren't, and somewhere in the chain you find the impossibility. The break is usually where nobody thought to look, because everyone stopped at the point where the story still sounded reasonable.

That is the part that stayed with me longest. Mathematics is one of the few places where the authority is the logic, not the person making the argument. Someone can tell you something with total confidence and you are still entitled to ask whether it follows. You learn early that being told an answer and understanding it are different events, and that only one of them is worth much.

It changed what I notice. I find it hard to be satisfied by what happened without pushing on why, and on whether the conclusion actually follows from the thing offered as evidence. When information arrives messy or incomplete, my instinct is to organize it before judging it. When a system misbehaves, I want to know which piece depends on which, and where the dependency was never made explicit. I'd rather test someone's framing than adopt it.

None of that is really about numbers. It is a temperament that mathematics happened to train, and it turns out most of the world rewards it — because most complicated situations are not actually mysterious. They are just unexamined. There is a structure underneath, and usually nobody has gone looking for it.

So the short version is this: I like understanding things. I like working them out for myself rather than being handed the conclusion. And I like the specific satisfaction of getting from this is confusing to now I see how it works — which, as far as I can tell, is the only thing I have ever really been chasing.

I think in business and operations now. But I'm a mathematician at heart, and I don't think that ever leaves you.
