# What Paris gave back to me

_Personal · Travel — Spring 2026 · Paris, France · 4 min read_

> I went to Paris for work. What I brought home had almost nothing to do with the work.

The project itself was familiar territory. I spent six years in consulting before business school, so the rhythm of it — structure the problem, interview the experts, build toward a recommendation — was a rhythm I already knew. What I did not expect was that the trip would hand something back to me that I had quietly misplaced.

Before Berkeley, I was curious by default. I liked discovering places, trying things, learning by wandering into them. Then I moved to the United States, and most of my energy went into adapting: a new country, a new school, a new way of doing ordinary things. Somewhere in all that adjusting, the exploratory part of me went quiet.

Paris brought it back.

It helped that the sun did not set until nearly ten. The evenings ran long and warm, and there was no natural cue to go inside, so I kept walking. I would finish work and walk for hours through neighborhoods and gardens for no reason other than wanting to see what was around the next corner. I had always thought of walking as the thing you do to get somewhere. In Paris it became the thing itself — time to turn the day over, notice buildings, let ideas settle. My favorite memories are not of the landmarks. They are of streets I had not planned to be on.

> Exploration does not always require a destination.

The other lesson was slower to arrive, and it was about slowing down. In interviews, in workshops, in presentations, I noticed that speaking quickly did not make me clearer — it just made me faster. So I started pacing my words, organizing my thoughts before they left my mouth, and leaving space for ideas to land. Conversations got better almost immediately. It turns out people need a moment to receive things.

Most of what I loved about the trip was small. Trying escargots for the first time with my teammates. Sitting by the Seine after work, playing cards and watching people go by. Walking through the Jardin du Luxembourg. Taking the Metro and discovering that I genuinely enjoyed navigating a city on my own, including the parts where I got it wrong and had to find my way back, and at every step of the way, I was grateful to those that came up with the live view feature of Google Maps.

That last one changed something. Before Paris I almost never took BART into San Francisco, on the assumption that I would not enjoy it. I had never tested the assumption. Since coming back, I take it — and I am far more willing to go toward places that feel unfamiliar.

I had wanted to see the Louvre for years, and standing inside it felt like closing a loop I had carried a long time. But my favorite museum turned out to be the Musée de l'Orangerie, which I had barely thought about beforehand. Monet's Water Lilies wrap around the room, and standing in front of them was the calmest I felt on the entire trip. It reminded me how much I need creative spaces, and how easily that need gets deprioritized.

The hardest part was the balance. There was always another neighborhood worth seeing, and always a deck to finish and a client expecting good work. Holding both taught me something about prioritizing honestly and using the time I actually had rather than the time I wished I had.

What I keep coming back to is that growth did not only happen in the client room. It happened while walking through a city with no particular destination, standing in front of a painting, sharing a plate of something unfamiliar with people I had come to like. Long after the deliverables have faded, I will remember the Water Lilies and the long evenings by the Seine — and the curiosity they woke back up, which I intend to keep feeding.
